# The onboarding interview

A resume gives you employers, titles and dates. It does not give you the three things that
actually determine whether this search produces good recommendations:

| Input | On a resume? | What it does downstream |
|---|---|---|
| Employment history, titles, dates | Yes | Baseline eligibility, seniority band |
| Target titles, level, compensation | Partly | Search scope |
| **Quantified outcomes** | Rarely | What fit analysis matches against. "Led initiatives" is unusable; "45% to 4%, verified against third-party records" survives an interview. |
| **Hard constraints** | No | Usually the single highest-leverage filter. Location alone can remove most of a market. |
| **Honest gap inventory** | Never | Generates the disqualifying gates. This is what stops bad applications. |

So: interview, do not just ingest. Work through the steps below, read the profile back in plain
language, get an explicit yes, and only then write `profile.json`. They keep the file and can
edit it as they learn.

**Order matters here.** Nothing is written until the user has confirmed it. Reading it back
routinely surfaces a correction they would never have volunteered, and a file written before
that correction is a file every later stage quietly trusts.

## Step 1 — Ingest whatever exists
Resume, LinkedIn URL, old cover letters, a performance review, a portfolio. Any one is enough to
start. Extract employers, titles, dates, scope.

## Step 2 — Reflect the timeline back
Show it. Then ask about unexplained gaps, roles left off, and anything early involving managing
people, budget or systems.

People routinely drop early roles they consider irrelevant. Those roles often contain the only
evidence of people management or operational scale in the whole history, and they are frequently
the most useful thing recovered in this step.

## Step 3 — Mine for numbers, one role at a time
For each recent role: what changed because you were there, and **how do you know?**

Push for the measurement method, not just the figure. A number the candidate cannot defend is
worse than no number, because it collapses under one follow-up question. If they cannot say how
they know, mark the claim soft and never lead with it.

## Step 4 — Hard constraints, asked explicitly
Relocation. On-site tolerance, and how many days. Travel ceiling. Visa status. Industries or
companies excluded. And compensation as **two separate numbers, floor and target**.

The floor never leaves this file. A candidate who states a floor to an employer is offered their
floor.

## Step 5 — Gap inventory
Ask directly, without judgment:

- What do these roles keep asking for that you do not have?
- What have you never actually done, whatever your resume implies?
- What interview question do you dread?

Record it verbatim. This is the section that prevents the tool from flattering, and it is the
only section no user will produce unprompted. Frame it honestly: naming a gap here is what stops
them wasting a month on a role that was never going to happen.

## Step 6 — Targets, then test them
Titles to include and exclude, and the level band.

**If they do not know what they want, do not start here.** "What role are you targeting?" is
unanswerable for a career-changer, and asking it first produces either a stall or a guess that
every later stage then treats as fact. For those users, run Steps 1 through 5 and then work
backwards: their outcomes and constraints usually name two or three title families on their own.
Propose those, with the evidence from their own history, and let them react. Reacting to a
concrete suggestion is far easier than generating one from nothing.

Then check the exclusions against reality rather than accepting them. Assumptions about level are
often wrong in ways the data settles quickly — a candidate may assume they need to target down a
level when almost no such roles exist remotely at their pay floor, which changes the whole
strategy.

## Step 7 — Read it back, then write it
Say the profile in plain language -- never raw JSON -- and wait for an explicit yes. Correct
what they correct. Write `profile.json` only after that yes. It is the input to everything
else, which is exactly why it does not get written on an assumption.

## When the user has nothing to hand
Some users arrive with no resume and no posting -- they have not started. There is still a
sequence, and it is not "come back when you have a resume."

Ask what they did most recently and who paid them for it. That single answer is enough to begin
Step 2. Build the timeline conversationally, one role at a time, and write it down for them as
you go: for many of these users the reconstructed timeline is itself the first deliverable, and
it is worth more than the ranking they came for.

Do not ask them to produce a document before you have given them something. A user who cannot
answer the first question tends to conclude the tool is not for them.

## When the user has very little
If they cannot answer Step 3, do not invent numbers. Mark every claim soft and make the first
deliverable a **list of things to go find out**: old performance reviews, a former manager to
ask, dashboards they may still have access to.

An honest profile with five solid facts beats a rich one with twenty unverifiable ones, because
the unverifiable ones fail in interviews rather than in the document.
